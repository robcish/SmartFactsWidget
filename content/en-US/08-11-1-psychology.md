In **Blocks World**, a program reasons about a table with colored blocks that can be stacked, moved, and rearranged. Terry Winograd's SHRDLU (1970) was the most famous system: you could type "Put the red block on top of the blue one" and it would plan and execute the moves.

The paradigm was seductive because it was tractable — a closed world with clear rules and no ambiguity. But this simplicity was also its downfall. Critics like Hubert Dreyfus argued that real intelligence doesn't work in neat, predefined domains. By the late 1970s, the AI community acknowledged that Blocks World success didn't transfer to the messy, open-ended real world.
