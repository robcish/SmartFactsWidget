In the summer of **1951**, Christopher Strachey wrote a draughts (checkers) program for the Ferranti Mark 1 at Manchester University. The program used the machine's entire memory — about 1,000 words — and could play a complete legal game from start to finish.

Strachey was not employed at the university; he was a schoolteacher who spent his holiday writing the program. He brought it in on a Friday evening and had it running by Monday. Alan Turing himself was so impressed that he gave Strachey access to the machine. It was one of the earliest demonstrations that computers could handle complex strategic reasoning.
