Louis Braille published his system in **1829**, encoding the French alphabet into patterns of 1–6 raised dots within a 2×3 cell. Readers perceive these patterns through fingertips rather than eyes, yet they achieve reading speeds of 100–200 words per minute — comparable to sighted reading of print.

This challenges the assumption that writing is inherently visual. Braille shows that what matters is **spatial contrast** — distinct patterns arranged in sequence. The same principle underlies all writing systems: distinguishable marks in a consistent spatial framework. Whether those marks are ink on paper or bumps on a page, the linguistic function is identical.
